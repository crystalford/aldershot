The manual

How Aldershot works

You say what you’re making. Aldershot plans the film, goes through the footage you have, finds what’s missing, and puts it together. You decide; it shows you why it did each thing. If it can check a fact, it checks it — and if it can’t find a source, it tells you.

Two of the examples below are live — the same engine as the Studio, running on the sample vlogs.

1

Start with a sentence

Home is where you say what you want to make, in your own words — “a twenty-minute essay about NASA as a medium”, “cut my three vlogs about moving down to six minutes”. Aldershot reads it as one of six kinds and opens the right project. Attach footage and it reads that too.

3

Direct it, and let it work

You don’t have to do it shot by shot. Give Aldershot a direction the way you’d give it to an editor — “make act two feel like an old documentary, NASA footage wherever it exists, a painted depiction for Kepler’s dream” — and it answers with a plan: the steps, which shots, what it costs. Press Go and carry on with anything else while the crew works through it, with the list beside the conversation showing where it’s up to.

Rules make the cut

A cut from your footage isn’t stored. It’s made fresh from a few plain sentences every time one changes. Switch one off and everything it did comes back; say a new one and it joins the list. When two disagree, the later one wins — and every moment says which rule put it there.

7

Ways to tell a scene

A shot can hold more than one picture. Open a scene and Aldershot pitches ways to tell it, made from what that scene contains — a countdown montage, the document read word by word, five months in fifteen seconds. Or describe it your way, in a sentence, and it becomes a shot list. Ways to tell the film does this across every scene at once, and never the same way twice in a row.

8

Scout finds what’s missing

Scout is how a film gets better than your footage alone. It looks for what each part needs — the proof behind a claim, the other side, the original a clip came from, someone else making the same point — in archives, libraries, podcasts and the open web. It links what it finds and checks whether it can be used. It never downloads anything.

9

Your voice, twenty minutes at a time

The film plays from the first minute, with every line you haven’t recorded yet shown as words. Today picks a sitting — about twenty minutes, whole sections, so you finish things. Press Record on any line and the take opens with that line, a countdown, and whether it fit its space. Keep it or go again.

Or record the whole script first. Open Script and choose Record the whole script: one line at a time, in film order, with the line before and after it on screen. Space starts and stops, Enter keeps the take and moves on. Each shot then follows your reading and keeps the pause it was planned with, so the pictures are cut to your voice rather than the other way round. If you say a line differently from how it’s written, it offers to change the line to what you said.

Nothing is thrown away. Every take of a line is kept, and so is every picture a shot has had. Open a shot to see its earlier versions and takes, and pick one to put it back.

Scene by scene is the same film, one scene at a time: its rough cut on the left, what it’s becoming on the right, and under them a card for every shot with the one thing to do next. Finish a scene and it says so, and offers the next.

10

Before it goes out

Export opens a check, in plain sentences: what’s still a placeholder, which lines aren’t in your voice, any claim with nothing behind it, whose footage it uses and how it’s credited, what was generated and how it’s labelled. Each comes with the fix that matters first. A rough cut can go out — labelled so nobody mistakes it for the finished film.

The package downloads as one file. The timeline opens in Premiere and Resolve, so you can always take the film somewhere else. Each shot arrives as a clip named for what it shows, with a marker saying where it came from, what it needs, and what backs any claim over it.

11

What it costs

Footage is counted once, the first time it’s read. Every cut, short and change after that is free — so try things. Generated pictures are the only thing that costs per use, and only what lands in a finished film counts. Video is made in clips of up to 8 seconds, at 36¢ a second (Fast) or $1.20 (Full quality), about three times what the model costs us, which pays for the tries you throw away. A longer shot is one clip that then holds on its last frame, a clip every 8 seconds, or a still that moves slowly, for 12¢. One monthly limit covers it all, and you set it.
