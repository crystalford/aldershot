WHAT’S CHANGED FROM ONE TAKE, IN ORDER
Names below are as they were at the time. On 20 Nov they changed to match the product’s glossary: Spine → Plan, Library → Footage, Rough film → Trailer, the Story editor → Aldershot.
  1. 01Gold only where it means youdone · 86 uses → 46
    Still gold — your voice and your takes, lines yours to record, “N of M recorded”, ghosts for you to grab, fixes for you to make before posting. One Take’s own rule: gold is you, to camera.
    No longer gold — links, Aldershot’s own message marks, the claim box, selected tabs, new-rule highlights, notes, “Go back here”, the sample hint. In-progress bars and “still needed” slots are cyan, One Take’s colour for the crew looking. And other people’s names on a transcript were gold while yours was grey — that’s flipped.
  2. 02A Library page for capturenew page · for your look
    Capture as a game you play by filling real gaps — and its shape follows the kind of video. It opens on the NASA documentary, where each section needs you saying it, something to show, and proof; switch to the vlog in the header and it becomes angles per thread. Both have a coverage map, one Scout mission per gap (each says what it gets you), the next unlock shown as a distance, every way in, and a trail of where captures ended up. Unlocks are things the Studio can do — no points, no badges. Now calm by default: one task on screen at a time, with how long it takes and what it gets you; the sections as a simple map you can open one at a time; everything else folded. Studio itself is untouched; the Library is reached from here for now. Open it →
  3. 03A rough film, drawn in codenew page · for your look
    The video plays from the first minute and sharpens as things arrive — now the whole essay, with a Drawn finish that is a complete motion-graphics cut on its own. Ghosts get an identity by type, and how finished a shot looks says how sure it is. Built to the standard of Anthropic’s IPO film: serif words landing one beat at a time, mono labels for what things are, a zoom that carries one shot into the next. Open it →
  4. 04The spine: structure at full lengthnew page · for your look
    The NASA essay at 20:00 — three acts, eleven sequences, each one raising or answering a question, each with its two-column script. The sound column is the spine: voiceover, sync, breathe, silence. Voiceover lines are prompts to record. A third crew member, the Story editor, proposes changes one at a time — like adding a sequence where your stream and your video disagree, then trimming to stay on length. Open it →
  5. 05The spine plays, and listensfor your look
    Every sequence plays as a rough film straight from its two-column script and runs on into the next — the whole 20 minutes, playable. Talk or type over it and the note pins at the playhead; Aldershot sorts it into a search for Scout, a drawing for Sketch, a move or trim for the Story editor, a timing change or a script rewrite. Next: the Studio, calmed down and rebuilt around all of this.
  6. 06The Studio, rebuilt as the hubfor your look
    Left: the whole film, playing as a rough cut, with its sequences over the sound weave and where you are in words. Right: the one thing waiting on you, and the crew — ask Story to open on Mariner 4 and the film reorders in front of you; ask Scout for the Apollo 1 bulletin and it lands in “The fire”; ask Sketch for the signal path and it plays. The detailed One Take editor is now the Editor tab. Open it →
  7. 07One film, one set of wordsfor your look
    Every tab reads and writes the same film — record a line on the Plan and the Studio’s waiting task moves on. The tabs are the same everywhere, in workflow order, and the names match the product’s glossary. Scout links and never downloads, so the copy that said he “pulls” a Twitch clip now says it’s linked at its source. The whole system is written up for the repo as a handoff.
  8. 08The new story, outside and infor your look
    The Landing now tells it: a live film drawn in code in the hero, the five steps, the two-column script, the crew, the Drawn finish, what it won’t do, and pricing — with Sign in finally linked. In the repo, the plan at film scale is built as real code (phase 1 of the handoff): stored as rules, computed into the film, with tests that reproduce this prototype’s own numbers. Open the Landing →
  9. 09One room, no timelinefor your look
    You said the tabs felt like five apps and the strips under the player were a timeline in disguise. Both were right. The Studio is now One Take’s room holding the whole film: the conversation on the left, the film top to bottom in the middle — sections that open in place and develop as the crew’s work lands — and NOW on the right, with no bar anywhere. Plan and Footage became lenses (Film, Script, Needs); the trailer is something you make from Also…; the vlog is the other project in the same room. Open it →
  10. 10A quieter room, and a morning reelfor your look
    You said the room was cluttered. Each section card now says one thing: its title, three dots (said · shown · proven) and the one word of status. The question appears only when you open a section; block rows lost their labels and let the colour of the rule say what you hear. The status lines, source chips, keyboard hint and progress panel are gone, and the suggestions are down to three. One thing was added: Watch what changed plays only the parts the crew touched while you were away — 22 seconds instead of twenty minutes.
  11. 11Record is a take, and takes chainfor your look
    Record used to tick a box. Now it opens the line in NOW, big, with a gold word walking it at a pace that fits the slot. 3·2·1, read it, Stop (or space). Aldershot measures the read against the slot and says it in words — fits with 3s to breathe, or 2s over — and recommends Keep or Again. Keep goes straight to Next line, with what this sitting has added and how many lines until the act is all in your voice. Starting is the expensive part, so you only start once.
  12. 12The essay underneath, and the Argument lensfor your look
    The sample film is rebuilt from the essay NASA as a medium — a probe about probes: 21 short sections, 20:00, each standing on the fact-check rows that back it. A new lens, Argument, shows the 34 claims with their verdicts, the doubter’s eight patterns, and the film laid against the claims — three checks with no model in them: claims with no section, sections with no claim, claims resting on attribution. A section’s proven dot only goes green when every claim under it is sourced. Open a section and its claims sit above the blocks; click one to jump to it. The Trailer still plays the first story; it’s next.
  13. 13Filling the shell: every slot can be madefor your look
    Every empty slot now has a Fill button. It opens the slot’s workbench on the right: what the slot needs, what’s said over it, whether it carries a claim, and four ways to fill it in the rules’ order — Yours (upload a real file, or a code to shoot it on your phone), Scout sources it, Sketch draws it, Generate it (switched off wherever a claim is spoken, with the reason). Each runs as a job you can watch or walk away from; failures say why and offer the next route. Progress is counted in film, never a percentage: Developed 5:39 of 20:00, with the crew’s work-in-progress and what’s waiting for your eye shown in the bar and on every section. Develop the film proposes a plan act by act — Sketch draws these, Scout looks for those, the shot is yours — and runs it in the background.
  14. 14Watching it developfor your look
    Filling now feels like something happening. While the crew works on a section, its thumbnail draws itself over and over and the block’s tile pulses. When the work lands, the block develops like a photograph — dark, grey and soft, then sharp — and a +0:20 rises beside the Developed count. Upload a picture and it becomes that section’s thumbnail. A clip brings its own sound, so “in their words” slots fill whole. And every act the crew develops ends with its own Watch what developed reel: only the slots that just landed, back to back, then “approve it, or redo any of it, right in the film.”
  15. 15A calmer room, and red for recordingfor your look
    You said it looked cluttered, and that record should be red. Recording is now red everywhere it appears: the Record buttons, the “Yours to record” card, the take — its Start button, its walking word, its slot bar. Amber only warns now (a take that runs long). The rest got quieter: two typefaces instead of three (the monospace is gone), no all-caps labels, one plain button style for every row with a small coloured dot saying what kind of action it is, cards without outlines, block tiles that show a small drawing instead of a word, and a film header that leads with one number — how much is developed — with your voice and the crew’s work in plain sentences under it.
  16. 16A real palette, and five steps for every sectionfor your look
    Amber and cyan are no longer the room’s colours. Each colour now means one thing: red is recording, green is done, blue is the crew working or what Aldershot recommends next, and everything else is ink — “yours” is white, not gold. And every section is now five steps in order — Written, Proven, Pictured, Voiced, Watched — shown when you open it, with a tick for each one done and a button only on the next. The card shows the five as small marks and says what’s next. Watched is earned by playing the section through. The header counts steps for the whole film: 49 of 105 to start, because the crew’s work counts.
  17. 17Today’s sittingfor the checklist you asked for
    The single “next thing” card is now a checklist for one sitting: never more than twenty minutes or eight lines, with how long each takes. Aldershot plans whole sections, the ones nearest done first — people speed up as a goal gets close, so finishing four sections beats nudging twelve — and always includes one line for you to say out loud. Only the line you’re on has a button. Lines tick themselves when the thing actually happens: a take kept, a find checked, a slot filled, a section watched — not when you tick a box. When the list is done it says how many sections you finished outright and offers a reel of just what you made today. Each act header now has a small strip, one mark per section: green when all five steps are done, blue for the one that’s next.
  18. 18Motion graphics, every kind of picture, and an editor for any blockfor “photo, clip, generative, motion graphic… and a way to edit”
    Sketch now has a library of nine motion graphics, each drawn in code from a few words: Signal, Broadcast, Counter, Noise to signal, Trajectory, Quote, Layers, Print-out, Timeline. Every drawn slot in the film starts as one of them with its own words — Sputnik’s rings, the 11:22 clock, Voyager’s noise settling, the orbit that misses — so the film plays as real graphics, even before they’re approved. Every block has an Edit (and NOW has Edit this block). The editor shows the block playing, then three things you can change. Picture: photo (with how it moves), clip (with its own sound if you want), found footage (Scout links it), motion graphic (pick a design, type the words, it redraws as you type), title card, or generated (drawn or painted, never photoreal; refused where the block carries a claim or is meant to show something real). Words: your voice, their words, breathe or silence, and the line itself — change a line you already recorded and it says so, with Record it again in red or Put the old words back. It tells you how long the words take to say. Length: a second at a time, with the film’s new total. Edits are saved as changes on top of the plan; Put it back as planned keeps your version so you can bring it back. Filling a slot now also offers a title card, and lets you choose Sketch’s design or write the generating prompt yourself.
  19. 19Every way to fill a slot — and moving things inside a graphicfor “all the possible ways… generative video too, if we can charge for it”
    The editor’s picture section is now every way there is, in five groups, ordered the way the rules order pictures. Yours: on camera, a clip, a photo (push in, drift, or punch in to a detail you click), your screen (recorded right there where the browser allows it), a document with the passage pulled out. Found: public archive, free stock, paid stock (licence at cost), quote a clip (Scout gives the link and the in and out; you bring the file). Drawn: motion graphic (now fourteen designs, adding bar chart, sound, receipt, captions and side by side), title card, receipt, side by side, captions only. Generated: a still, a video, animate a still of yours, restyle a clip of yours — drawn, painted, clay, paper or abstract, never photoreal, refused over a claim or anything shown as fact. Nothing, or again: a beat of black, hold the last frame, bring a shot back, and (designed, not built) you over it. The plan’s own choice is marked Recommended.

    Charging for generated video. Doc 10’s prices stand: $1 a second at Standard and $3.50 at Premium, 25¢ a still — billed only for seconds still in the film at export. November 2026 list prices put the model cost at about $0.03–0.16 a second for draft and standard models and $0.40–0.60 for premium, so those prices still cover three tries with margin. Drafts are free. Every price is shown before you make anything, in dollars for this block’s length; the Keep button says the price; a $50-a-month limit turns the line red when a pick would pass it — and the choice stays yours.

    Moving things in a graphic. In the editor, drag any words to move them; a blue corner resizes them; drag empty space to move the whole graphic and its corner to scale it. Positions are saved with the graphic and play the same in the film.
  20. 20The map, and the Studio folded downfor “zoom out… leaner and sleeker… Needs and Also are weird names”
    A new Map page (for us) counts every feature across all twelve screens, where it lives, where it should go, and what was lost. Then step one of it: the bar is now Film · To do · Script · Argument · Export. To do is the one list — milestones (the whole film plays → every slot pictured → every line in your voice → safe to publish), today’s sitting, the crew’s Develop, then everything else. The Film view lost its stack of cards and starts with the film, with one button that says how today is going. Export is one menu: the film, with three plain lines on what it would go out with, then what’s made from it — a trailer, shorts, a Drawn cut. Fill and Edit are one block panel: the crew’s progress, drafts, Scout’s candidates and failures all show inside it, and a draft plays in the panel’s own preview. “How this fits” left the product bar for the foot of the conversation, next to Map.
  21. 21Home and Librarystep two of the map
    Home is the place with no project open: “What are we making?”, a box you talk into (it reads the sentence and says which kind of video it sounds like, and opens it), the six kinds, your projects with real progress read from the same shared film, and a glance at the library. Library is everything across projects: your recordings and every moment in them, searchable by meaning or exact words; Scout’s finds by status, with credits; every graphic rendered from its own code; eight ways in, each saying whether it’s built; and where things went — every moment and piece, what it became, and the rule that cut it. The engine says something worth seeing there: the Sunday line where you decide to go isn’t in the cut, because no rule keeps it. The Studio’s logo now goes Home, the bar has Home and Library, and the project switcher links to all projects.
  22. 22Real pictures on every cardfor “can we do better than these images?”
    The little icons are gone. Every section card and every block now shows its own picture, drawn by the same code the film plays: the motion graphic as it ends (labels too small to read are dropped, the main words lifted), a title card set as type, Scout’s finds as an archive plate with what it is and whether it’s checked, your own lines as a camera frame with a waveform — solid and green when recorded, outlined with a red dot when not — and your uploads as themselves. A section shows its most telling block; while it plays, its card plays along. Anything not made yet sits back, faded and dashed, so you can see at a glance what’s real.
  23. 23Click a shot to change it · the scene strip · Rules and budget · what’s on topfor “I don’t see any way to do anything”
    Open a section and its shots are laid out in order, each as its own picture sized to its length, with how it comes in and a line under it for its sound — click any shot to change it. Every picture in the Studio is now the way into that shot. “Slots” are now shots. Checking a find or approving a drawing happens in the shot’s panel, where you can see the thing: “Sketch drew this. Is it right?” — Use it, or Draw it again. A new Rules view holds the film-wide choices: what the pictures can cost (Nothing · A little · Where it shows), how anything generated looks, credits, dissolves, captions, breathing room. Aldershot’s recommendation for every shot follows the budget and says why, and Develop makes them that way. Each shot’s panel gains On top, and in and out: a credit line, words on top, a name strap, captions, you small in a corner, and how it comes in and goes out (cut, fade, dissolve, dip to black) — the defaults from Rules, any of them changeable for one shot. That’s the whole of “layers” here: a picture, what’s on it, and how it arrives and leaves.
  24. 24Ways to tell a scenefor the countdown montage, “as just one example”
    A shot can now hold a sequence. Open a section and “Other ways to tell it” opens the Scene panel: the scene playing, and the ways Aldershot would tell it — as planned, its own pick, words that move, a montage of what the scene echoes — each with why, the devices it uses, how many cuts and what Scout has to find. Play any of them above, then “Tell it this way”. Or say how you see it in your own words and it becomes a shot list: Chris’s countdown (“glitch shots of the Moon landing… Parker Solar Probe… theatre… 1–2 seconds each, about 20 seconds, promo-style words”) turns into 13 cuts, a countdown, glitches, a beep on every cut and a closing line. Fifteen devices are named — montage, countdown, glitch, beeps, words that move, side by side, archive reveal built; match cut, read it on screen, cutaways, let it breathe, speed ramp, cut to music, one-frame flash described. The model chooses the devices and sources; code places every cut. Until Scout links the real footage, each cut shows what it’s waiting for — and the beeps are real.
  25. 25Three more ways to tell a scene, and a pass over the whole filmstep on from the montage
    Built: read it on screen (the document itself, its passage lit word by word as it’s read, the camera pushing in — McLuhan’s 1977 letter), speed ramp (a long stretch run at speed with its clock racing, then real time on the moment — Voyager’s five months from November to 20 April; the 11:22 the landing signal takes), and cut to music (a picture on every bar at 96 bpm, with a soft thump on each beat — the world hearing Sputnik). Every scene now gets pitches made from its own shots — read it where there’s a document, cut to music where there’s a long breath. Ways to tell the film (on the Film view) is Aldershot’s pass over all 21 scenes: it tells 10 differently — strong fits always, lighter ones only after two plain scenes, never the same way twice in a row — and shows the film’s rhythm before and after as two strips. See any one, tell it that way, or take them all.
  26. 26The Studio, led by footagephase 3 — rules, and cutting your own recordings
    “Working out whether to move” now opens in the Studio — the same room, led by footage instead of a script. Its lenses are Cut, Words, Footage, Shorts and Rules, all made by One Take’s engine (footage.js) every time anything changes; the cut itself is never stored. The sample comes out as before — 8 of 14 moments, 5:59, 4 spaces to camera. Every moment says which rule kept it, and everything left out says what left it out, with Put it back. Aldershot opens by noticing the decision on Sunday at 44:02 isn’t in, because no rule keeps where you land it, and offers to keep it; then Scout offers a podcast making the same trade, linked and credited. Say a change and it becomes a rule you can read and switch off (“cut the house”, “under four minutes”, “start on the sharpest line”, “keep the line about the room”); ask a question and it answers with the timed lines (“when did I talk about money?” — four times, two in the cut). Anything it can’t turn into a rule says so. Every change has Undo, and ⌘Z. Rules shows what each rule is doing and what switching it off would do. Footage lays the threads out by day and says what each is missing. Shorts bands each moment Strong, Worth a look or Long shot with the lines that put it there. The spaces to camera are takes with the thread’s own lines as prompts. Home, the Library’s “Where things went” and the Studio’s project switcher read the same saved rules.
  27. 27Settings, the Manual, and a real export checkphase 4
    Settings is rebuilt in the Studio’s look, with one spending limit (it was $50 in the Studio and $60 in Settings — now it’s whatever you set, and the Studio reads it), the plans from doc 10, connected services you can switch, how new work starts, storage, and the thirty-day pile with Restore. Export, in both kinds of project, now opens Before it goes out: every line from doc 09 as a plain sentence — pictures, your voice, claims, other people’s footage, generated pictures, captions, sound, receipts — each with the fix that matters first, and a verdict (ready, a rough cut, or not yet). Download the package makes a real .zip from the project as it is: an edit list Resolve and Premiere open (the cut’s points at your three recordings by their own timecodes), captions, credits, receipts, the cue sheet, the ledger of what was left out, and the provenance manifest. Rendering the video is the one thing the canvas can’t do, and it says so. The Manual is rewritten for the Studio — eleven chapters, two of them live: switch the sample rules and watch the cut re-make itself; ask the three vlogs anything and get the lines back, timed. Small things: Aldershot’s recommendations are the same quiet blue everywhere; Escape closes the check.
  28. 28The final pass: direct it, scene by scene, and a landing that shows itbefore the repo
    Direct it. Give the Studio a direction in a sentence — “make act two feel like an old documentary, NASA footage wherever it exists, a painted depiction for Kepler’s dream” — and it answers with a plan: the steps, which shots, the cost. Go, and the crew works through it with a live list by the conversation; at the end a reel of what it did, what needs your eye, and three suggested next directions. Depictions. The wall that said a claim “needs its receipt, not an invention” is now a rule: Depict it, labelled (the default — a generated picture can show what’s being said, labelled “Depiction” on screen, and the claim keeps its receipt) or Strict. This changes invariant 8; the repo port changes 13-DECISIONS and CLAUDE.md in the same PR. The shot ladder. The shot panel no longer shows 22 ways at once: every shot starts as a sketch, and four moves — keep it drawn, find the real thing, use yours, generate from it — open their options only when picked. Designs and “on top, and in and out” fold away. Scene by scene, a new surface: one scene at a time, its rough cut beside what it’s becoming, a card per shot with the one next thing, and a quiet celebration when a scene is finished. The landing is rebuilt: a film developing in the hero, the cold open before and after (the drawn rough beside the finished montage on the beat, with what Scout found), three ways forward from the rough cut (keep building, take it to your editor, hand it to an agent over MCP — designed), directing, the ten promises with what checks each, and doc 10’s plans.
  29. 29Voice first, and every version keptfrom the research pass
    Animated films record the voice before they draw, and the read sets the timing. The Studio now works that way. Record the whole script (in Script, in To do, or just say it) opens a booth: one line at a time in film order, the lines either side of it, Space to start and stop, Enter to keep and move on. Each shot then follows your reading and keeps the pause it was planned with. If you say a line differently from how it’s written, it offers to change the line to what you said. With a microphone, you can hear a take back, or every take in order; without one, a take is a timed read. To do now asks for your voice before pictures, and so do the five steps of a section. Nothing replaced is gone: every take of a line, and every picture a shot has had — the plan, a drawing, a find, each generation — is kept on the shot, and one click puts it back. The landing copy was also rewritten in plain words, without the font switches, and the made-up before-and-after moved to its own concept page until there’s a real scene to show.
  30. 30Short generated shots, priced from what they costfrom the research pass
    Generated video is made in clips of up to 8 seconds; that’s the limit of Veo 3.1 and the models like it. A shot longer than that now asks how to fill the time: one clip, then hold on its last frame; a clip every 8 seconds, each a new angle, cut together; or a still that moves. Each shows its price, and the film plays it that way (the hold freezes, the clips cut). Prices now come from what the model costs, at about three times it (decision 38): Fast is 36¢ a second (it costs us 12¢), Full quality $1.20 (it costs us 40¢), a still 12¢. It was $1 and $3.50 a second and 25¢ a still. The three times pays for the tries you throw away, which stay free, and you still pay only for what’s in the film when you export. A 30-second shot that was $30 is now $2.88 as one clip and a hold. (For a day this page said half again instead of three times; at that, two free drafts before a keep cost more than the keep paid.) The Settings, Manual and landing prices match.
  31. 31A timeline your editor opens, with the sources on itfrom the research pass
    The package now has a timeline file (Final Cut 7 XML, the one format Premiere and Resolve both import). In the essay it holds all 49 shots on the video track, your recorded lines on the audio track, and a marker on every shot saying what it is, where it came from, whether it’s still a placeholder, and the line over it; each section and each claim gets a marker too, with the claim’s receipt or “nothing backs this”. The shots arrive offline, named for their place in the film and what they show, in a folder for their kind, ready to relink. In a footage-led project the clips point at your three recordings at the right timecodes, each with a marker saying which rule kept it. Resolve doesn’t bring markers in with the XML, so a markers file goes with it in the form Resolve imports (Timeline › Import › Timeline Markers from EDL), colour-coded, one marker per frame because Resolve keeps only one. The README says how to open both. Checked by reading the files back with OpenTimelineIO: 20:00 and 49 clips for the essay, 5:59 and 12 clips for the cut.
