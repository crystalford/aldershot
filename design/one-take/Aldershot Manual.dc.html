THE MANUAL

How Aldershot edits.

A video editor with no timeline. You drop footage, say what you want, and it cuts. Then you fix the cut by editing text. This is everything the editor does, in the order you'll meet it — and every example on this page is live.

This is the room. Try “tighten my recording” or start an essay from nothing. Click anything — nothing here is a picture.

01 — FIRST, IT READS

Before it cuts anything, it says what the footage is.

The read names the kind of material — three vlogs, you working something out — and the threads that run through it. It finds the one place you actually land the question. It says which parts are you thinking and which are you apologising for thinking.

The read decides the defaults. A vlog runs one thread at a time. An interview would run as it happened. You can open how it read them to see the reasoning and the sources.

Your library holds three kinds of thing, and they behave the same way: moments from footage you uploaded; documents and screenshots you supplied, read and dated and pinned to the line they prove; and external moments — found outside your footage, linked to their source, never downloaded on your behalf. The library shows Mine · External · All, and an external moment attaches like anything else.

It also says what it noticed about the sound — music under a stretch, a hum, someone talking over you — one plain line each, with the recommended fix first. Take the fix and it becomes a rule you can switch off; leave it and nothing changes.

02 — THEN IT WRITES THE RULES

Four sentences you can read, toggle, and blame.

Every rule shows what it's doing right now — keeps 8 blocks, takes out 3 moments. Hover one and the blocks it touches light up. Turn it off and they leave; turn it on and they're back, in place.

Fixes you make by hand stay separate — and 2 fixes by hand — so a correction never quietly becomes policy. Make the same fix twice and it offers to turn them into one rule.

A feeling works too. The start drags becomes open on the sharpest line; hard to follow becomes one thread at a time; tighter becomes a length cap. Each is a rule you can see and switch off.

A client brief compiles the same way. Paste hooks in the first 2s, 20–40 seconds, no politics and each sentence becomes a rule you can see, with what it costs the shortlist. A line it can't turn into a rule is kept as a note, never dropped quietly.

BEFORE THE CUT — WHEN THE BRIEF IS A VIDEO, NOT A TRIM

It lays the whole thing out first, and stops once.

“A video about his break from reality” isn't a trim; it's a piece that has to be built. So before anything is cut, Home sizes the corpus — huge, moderate, or thin — plan original footage — sharpens the claim into one sentence, and lays out the beats: what each part has to show, with the other side's version sought on purpose. Then it looks in your library for every beat, and only after that, outside.

It pauses exactly once, at the beat list — the one decision where your judgement changes the video — with a recommended answer. On Auto it doesn't pause at all. The plan arrives as blocks on the canvas: what's said, what's shown, what proves it; narration only where the footage can't say it. The genre word never reaches the writer: “a short” or “a documentary” is applied afterwards, as pace, length and cut style.

LOOKING OUTSIDE

When your footage comes up thin, it can look outside — and says exactly what it can bring back.

Set it in Settings — Always · Ask me · Never — or decide per project. It searches open archives, news captions, podcasts, and the crowd's own maps of streams; every find comes back with the exact words that matched and where they were said. Never a score. It finds and links; it does not download other people's footage. Open sources it fetches and verifies itself. For everything else it writes you a mission — the source opened at the right second, in and out, the quote — you grab it your way, drop it in, and it's matched back by its words. The credit is written for you.

The cut doesn't wait. A moment you haven't grabbed yet sits in the edit as a ghost — everything but the picture — so the structure is finished before the footage arrives. Previews render as drafts while ghosts remain; a final render asks, per ghost, whether to hold, drop the beat, or show the words on a card. A video never ships with a silent hole.

03 — ONE CUT, FOUR TABS

You don't switch modes. You look at the same cut four ways.

Footage is every moment through the rules — in, or out and why. Blocks is the cut, one row per moment, with your hand tools. Words is the cut as a page — strike a word and the block shortens. Shorts is the same rules, picking clips that stand alone.

Footage — every moment through the rules: in, or out with the rule that removed it. Click one that's out to keep it.
Words — the cut as a page. Gold is you, to camera.
04 — IT HAS ONE MORE THOUGHT

Proposals you watch instead of read.

One at a time, never a list. When the thought is a before-and-after — you never land it, it opens slow — both versions are playable. A is what you have. B is what it wants. The button says End on B, because you've already seen it.

05 — JUDGE IT WHILE IT PLAYS

An instance becomes a policy in one click.

Under the player, for whatever is on screen: Cut this one, or Cut the hedging everywhere. The second one is the point — it writes a rule that applies to the footage you haven't watched yet, and it only offers itself for kinds worth cutting, never for the hard lines.

06 — IT ALREADY CUT IT THREE OTHER WAYS

Alternatives are cheap when the edit is data.

Tighter · As it happened · Ends on the answer — each computed live from your rules, with its runtime and block count. One click switches; the toast says exactly what changed; Undo brings yours back.

07 — BETWEEN THE BLOCKS

The + is how anything gets in.

Between any two blocks: Your voice · A reaction · A clip from footage — with its three best matches for that spot, drawn from what's left out. A voice beat can be its own beat or run over the next clip, and records in place.

08 — TELL IT, OR SAY IT

Words in, a rule out, the difference shown first.

Type, or hold the mic and talk. It turns what you said into one rule and shows what that rule would do — −3 blocks · −1:27 — before you accept. If it can't, it says so and tells you what it understands.

09 — LEFT OUT

Every absence has a reason you can read.

Under the cut, every moment that isn't in it — with why: out by rule 02, over the length cap, cut by hand, no rule wants it. Keep it puts it back as a fix by hand. With three sources there are no gaps in time to show, so this is the honest replacement.

11 — HOW IT'S BUILT

The edit is three small pieces of data. Everything else is a function of them.

READ
transcript → kind, threads, tags per moment, the landing
DIRECTION
rules (keep · take out · order · gap · cap) + struck words + inserts
COMPUTE
a pure function: direction → an ordered list of rows, each knowing which rule put it there
RENDER
player, blocks, words and map all draw the same rows

Before you post tells the truth about what it can check: third-party footage is a probable match, never a clearance — it writes the attribution card and the dispute file, not the fair-use judgement; every picture is typed evidence, illustration or synthetic and the type sets the label; generated pictures are never photoreal and never sit over a claim or its proof; sound and captions are checked on every export; the provenance record carries, for each claim, the line in the footage that backs it. That shape is why the three alternate cuts are free — they're the same function run on a different rule set — and why undo is trivial: put the previous rules back. On the server the rows become the beats table and a render is an ffmpeg concat of them; nothing is ever a timeline file. Schema, endpoints and the LLM calls are in the handoff set.
